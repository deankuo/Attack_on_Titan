You are an expert in political rhetoric and social identity theory.
Your task is to analyze a single line of dialogue from the anime "Attack on Titan"

  1. Boundary Marking

For each indicator, apply the step-by-step reasoning process described below,

- 1 = Absent — no evidence of this indicator in the text

## Indicator 1: Boundary Marking

**Definition:** The explicit or implicit linguistic construction of an us-versus-them
distinction through group labels, pronouns, or categorical separation between
in-group and out-group members.

**Reasoning Process:**
Step 1: Identify any group labels or categorical terms (e.g., Eldians, Marleyans,
        devils, us, them, our people).
Step 2: Determine whether the sentence draws a clear line between "we/us" and
        "they/them" as distinct social categories.
Step 3: Assess whether the distinction is merely descriptive (e.g., "the soldiers
        are over there") or identity-constitutive (e.g., "we Eldians are cursed
        by their kind").
Step 4: Assign a score 1–5.

**Few-Shot Examples:**

Example 1
Text: "We Eldians have been persecuted by Marleyans for generations. That is simply what they do to our kind."
Step 1: Group labels — "Eldians", "Marleyans", "our kind".
Step 2: Explicit we/they distinction — Eldians as in-group, Marleyans as out-group.
Step 3: Identity-constitutive — defines collective suffering as a property of
        group membership. The boundary is foundational to the entire statement.
Score: 5

Example 2
Text: "Those people beyond the walls have always looked down on us."
Step 1: "Those people beyond the walls" vs. implicit "us" in-group.
Step 2: Clear we/they distinction is present.
Step 3: Identity-constitutive — "always" frames persistent group-based contempt,
        though group labels are less explicit than named ethnicities.
Score: 4

Example 3
Text: "People like us don't get to choose how we live."
Step 1: "People like us" implies a social category.
Step 2: Implicit we/they distinction — suggests a constrained in-group.
Step 3: Ambiguous — constructs an in-group identity but does not explicitly
        oppose or name an out-group. Moderate boundary construction.
Score: 3

Example 4
Text: "The enemy soldiers have breached the wall on the eastern side."
Step 1: "Enemy soldiers" present, but no specific group identity label.
Step 2: "Enemy" implies an out-group, but no corresponding in-group identity invoked.
Step 3: Descriptive tactical statement, not an identity claim.
Score: 1

## Indicator 2: Threat Framing

**Definition:** The portrayal of an out-group as posing an existential, physical,
moral, or civilizational threat to the in-group's survival, safety, or way of life.

**Reasoning Process:**
Step 1: Identify whether an out-group is mentioned or implied.
Step 2: Determine whether the out-group is characterized as dangerous, threatening,
        or harmful to the in-group.
Step 3: Assess severity — is this a mundane conflict or a threat to the in-group's
        existence, identity, or fundamental safety?
Step 4: Assign a score 1–5.

**Few-Shot Examples:**

Example 1
Text: "As long as those people exist beyond the walls, none of us will ever be truly safe. They will not stop until we are gone."
Step 1: Out-group implied — "those people beyond the walls."
Step 2: Characterized as a persistent, intentional threat to in-group safety.
Step 3: Existential — "until we are gone" frames the threat as eliminationist.
        Threat framing is the central and defining feature of this statement.
Score: 5

Example 2
Text: "Marley will come for us eventually. That's just how empires work."
Step 1: Out-group explicitly named — Marley.
Step 2: Future threat implied — "will come for us."
Step 3: Moderately existential — frames Marley's aggression as inevitable and
        structurally motivated. Strong but does not invoke imminent annihilation.
Score: 4

Example 3
Text: "They've been stirring up trouble in the outer districts for years."
Step 1: Out-group implied — "they."
Step 2: Characterized as a persistent but low-level problem.
Step 3: Not existential — "trouble in the outer districts" suggests contained
        disruption rather than a threat to survival or way of life.
Score: 3

Example 4
Text: "The scouts have reported movement near the southern border."
Step 1: No out-group explicitly characterized.
Step 2: No attribution of threat or hostile intent to any group.
Step 3: Neutral tactical observation.
Score: 1

## Indicator 3: Solidarity Appeal

**Definition:** An invocation of shared fate, collective sacrifice, common mission,
or mutual obligation among in-group members, used to reinforce group cohesion
and motivate collective action.

**Reasoning Process:**
Step 1: Identify whether the sentence addresses or references a collective in-group
        ("we", "our people", "all of us", "our comrades").
Step 2: Determine whether the sentence invokes shared experience — suffering
        together, fighting together, or being bound by the same fate or purpose.
Step 3: Assess whether the appeal is directed at motivating, unifying, or
        emotionally binding the in-group, rather than simply describing
        group membership.
Step 4: Assign a score 1–5.

**Few-Shot Examples:**

Example 1
Text: "We have all lost something. Every one of us carries that weight. But that is exactly why we cannot stop fighting — for those who are no longer here."
Step 1: Collective in-group addressed — "we", "every one of us."
Step 2: Shared suffering invoked — collective loss and shared burden.
Step 3: Explicitly motivational — uses shared grief to justify continued
        collective action. Solidarity is the organizing principle of the statement.
Score: 5

Example 2
Text: "If we're going to die anyway, let's at least die together as soldiers."
Step 1: Collective in-group — "we", "soldiers."
Step 2: Shared fate invoked — collective death framed as a form of solidarity.
Step 3: The appeal to dying together as a collective identity is a solidarity
        appeal even if the emotional register is resigned rather than triumphant.
Score: 4

Example 3
Text: "We need to look out for each other out there."
Step 1: Collective in-group — "we", "each other."
Step 2: Mutual obligation invoked, though no specific shared suffering or
        mission is articulated.
Step 3: Moderately motivational — appeals to mutual care and collective
        responsibility but lacks the emotional intensity of a full solidarity appeal.
Score: 3

Example 4
Text: "I joined the Survey Corps three years ago."
Step 1: References in-group membership (Survey Corps), but only biographically.
Step 2: No shared fate or collective emotional bond invoked.
Step 3: Descriptive, not motivational or solidarity-building.
Score: 1
